Q: How does CRISPR-Cas9 gene editing work?

A: CRISPR-Cas9 is derived from a natural bacterial immune defense system, in which bacteria capture fragments of viral DNA and store them as CRISPR arrays, later producing RNA segments that recognize and target invading viruses if they attack again [7]. In laboratory use, this system is simplified into a genome editing tool by combining the Cas9 nuclease with a synthetic guide RNA (gRNA) that is designed to match a specific DNA sequence [1,5]. The guide RNA directs Cas9 to the targeted location in the genome, where the enzyme cleaves both strands of the DNA [5,6]. Once the DNA is cut, the cell’s natural repair mechanisms take over, either rejoining the ends in a way that introduces insertions or deletions (often disrupting the gene) or allowing researchers to add, remove, or replace specific sequences [5,6]. A single guide RNA can be used to create one cut leading to gene inactivation, while more advanced approaches, such as using homology-directed repair (HDR) donor templates, allow precise insertion of new genetic material, such as tags for studying protein function, though this method's efficiency is often limited by low knock-in rates [2,6].
