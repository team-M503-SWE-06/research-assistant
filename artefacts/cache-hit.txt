Q: How does CRISPR-Cas9 gene editing work?

A: CRISPR-Cas9 is derived from a bacterial antiviral defense system that has been adapted into a genetic engineering tool for editing genomes [1,7]. The process relies on two main components: a synthetic guide RNA (gRNA) and the Cas9 enzyme [1,6]. The guide RNA is designed to match a specific target DNA sequence and directs the Cas9 protein to that exact location in the genome [1,5,7]. Once positioned, Cas9 acts as molecular "scissors," cutting the DNA at the targeted site [6,7]. This break in the DNA allows the cell's own repair machinery to remove or add genetic material, enabling scientists to knock out genes, correct mutations, or insert new sequences [1]. For more advanced applications, such as precise gene tagging, researchers can enhance editing efficiency using techniques like antibiotic resistance-based selection to improve homology-directed repair (HDR) outcomes [2].

A: CRISPR-Cas9 is adapted from a bacterial antiviral defense system, originally used by bacteria to cut up the DNA of invading viruses [1,7]. In gene editing applications, scientists design a synthetic guide RNA (gRNA) that is complexed with the Cas9 nuclease enzyme and delivered into a cell [1,5]. This precise cut allows existing genes to be removed or disrupted, or—when combined with a donor DNA template—new genetic sequences to be inserted via homology-directed repair (HDR), enabling applications such as protein tagging in human cells [1,2]. Overall, this two-molecule system (Cas9 enzyme plus guide RNA) gives researchers a programmable way to alter DNA sequences and modify gene function [5,6].
